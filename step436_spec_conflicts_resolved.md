# Step 436 — conflicts between his own videos, and how each is resolved

The specs are extracted from about 310,000 words spanning roughly three
years, so he contradicts himself in places. Every contradiction is recorded
here with the resolution and the reason, so nobody has to re-litigate it and
nobody can quietly pick the convenient version later.

**The standing rule: when his newer teaching contradicts his older teaching,
the newer wins.** He is a working trader who changed his method; the older
video is not more authoritative for being more detailed. Wallace flagged the
age gap himself when he sent the course: *"3 years old but should include
most of what you need."*

## 1. Order blocks: RETIRED. Do not build them.

**The conflict.** Three bootcamp days (20, 22, 24) teach order blocks and
rank them as the FIRST choice for where to enter on a pullback. His newest
equilibrium video says, verbatim:

> "I no longer use order blocks. I no longer use breaker blocks. The only
> continuation confluences that I need are equilibrium and fair value gaps."

His stated reason: the equilibrium level is usually getting filled at the
same moment anyway, so the order block adds nothing.

**Resolution: order blocks are OUT of the build.** Newer wins, and he does
not merely prefer the alternative, he says he stopped using them. The
bootcamp material stays documented in step432 as history.

**Why this matters more than it looks.** Order blocks are three bootcamp
days of detailed instruction — the most thoroughly taught thing in that
cluster. Building the most-documented concept would have been the natural
move, and it would have been building a method he abandoned. This is the
exact failure the whole extraction exists to prevent.

So the pullback entry is: **equilibrium and fair value gaps. Nothing else.**

## 2. What confirms a swing high or low: ANSWERED, no gap

**The conflict.** The confluences spec flags this as its single biggest
hole — he anchors nearly everything to "the most recent swing high/low" and
never mechanically defines one, doing it by eye every time. Since trend
state, both equilibrium anchors and break of structure all sit on top of it,
that hole would have sunk the build.

**Resolution: it is already answered, in a video that agent did not read.**
The liquidity videos define it mechanically and repeatedly:

> A high is an up candle followed by a down candle, and the level is the
> HIGHER of those two candles' wicks. A low is a down candle followed by an
> up candle, at the LOWER wick.

That is the definition, it is his, and it is stated plainly. Round 370 and
round 410 both measured it against the fractal definition this project had
been using and found it sits **35-44% closer to price on every one of 12
instruments tested** — which roughly doubles the position a fixed dollar
risk buys.

**Use the two-candle definition everywhere.** It is the single anchor under
trend state, both equilibrium points, order flow, and break of structure.
No gap here.

## 5. Fair value gaps, settled precisely

Three consecutive candles, and the middle one is the only one whose colour
matters:

- **Bullish gap**: the LOW of the third candle is above the HIGH of the
  first. The box runs between those two prices.
- **Bearish gap**: the HIGH of the third candle is below the LOW of the
  first.
- Overlapping wicks means there is no gap.
- **It dies on a candle CLOSING through it, never on a wick poking in.**
- It is also discarded once price has continued the trend without ever
  coming back to it, and it is never dragged forward in time.

**Gap inversion** — that same close-through — is what he uses INSTEAD of
waiting for a break of structure, and its entire value is that it fires
earlier. He quantifies it twice on the chart: 68 ticks and 123 ticks of
better entry, which turned a trade making half of what it risked into ones
making 1.3 and 2 times what they risked. In his words, risking $1,000 to
make $450 versus risking $1,000 to make $1,300.

On a stack of gaps it must be the BOTTOM gap for a bullish setup and the TOP
gap for a bearish one.

## 6. Equilibrium, settled precisely

The exact halfway point between the **most recent** swing low and the **most
recent** swing high (reversed in a downtrend). Below it is cheap, above it
is expensive. Buy below, sell above.

He spends five unbroken minutes on one error: **anchoring to an earlier
swing instead of the most recent one.** With the two-candle definition
above, "most recent" is now mechanical rather than a judgement call, which
is what makes this codeable at all.

## 7. Position size: take his taught method, not his personal shortcut

**The conflict is inside his own practice.** He teaches: risk **1% of the
account** per trade, in a band of 1% to 3%, calculated fresh each time from
where the stop sits. That is arithmetically identical to the rule this
project already uses — dollars risked divided by stop distance, leverage
falling out as an output. His worked example: a $100,000 account, a 4-point
stop, risking 1% of the account, 10 units per lot, gives 25 lots.

But what he PERSONALLY does is different: a **fixed lot size calibrated to
his narrowest typical stop**. The consequence is that on a day when the stop
has to sit further away, he does not size down — he risks 2% or 3% of the
account on that trade instead.

**RESOLUTION REVERSED, 2026-07-25. Build HIS method: the fixed size.**

The original resolution here was to calculate fresh every trade, and I
justified it by saying the fixed-lot version gives the most money to the
trades with the widest stops, which I called backwards for an account this
size.

That was me overruling a professional trader on his own method, using
reasoning I invented. Wallace:

> "this has nothing to do with me or you, but with tjr"

And earlier, on exactly this pattern:

> "you and i are not professionals. tjr is. if you were a professional you
> would be profitable already. there is no disagreeing with him, only
> testing it when you find the opputunity"

**It is also not a shortcut he happens to use — he teaches it, on the
whiteboard, as the method**, in the bootcamp day devoted entirely to
position size:

> "you're going to set your stop loss in points — what's usually the LOWEST
> your stop loss will be during a trade. For me it's usually 400. We
> calculate that, boom, 25 lots. That is going to be our SET lot size. And
> you're probably saying, well that doesn't make any sense — well it does,
> because that means I'm going to be risking one percent if price hits stop
> right here. That also means I'll be risking two percent of my account if
> we have a larger stop loss."

So the rule is:

1. Take the **narrowest stop the instrument normally produces** (re-derived
   per instrument from its own recent setups, never ported — Bitcoin's
   narrowest and SPY's are different numbers permanently).
2. Size so that stop risks **1% of the account**.
3. **Hold that size.** A wider stop that day therefore risks 2% or 3%. Do
   not resize down.
4. Recompute the fixed size as equity changes, and re-derive the narrowest
   stop periodically — but never per trade.

This answers the 1%-versus-3% question, which was never a choice. His method
produces 1% on the tightest setups and up to 3% on the widest, automatically.
The band is an OUTPUT of the rule, not a dial.

The three-tier scaling is unchanged and still applies on top: half size on
news days and holidays, normal size otherwise, and the double-size tier
**stays disabled** — he explicitly forbids it to anyone without a proven
record, and we have none.

The three-tier scaling he describes is kept as stated: **half size** on news
days and holidays, **normal size** otherwise, and **double size** on high
conviction — which he explicitly forbids to anyone without a proven record,
so it ships disabled and stays disabled.

## 8. The losing-streak rule REPLACES our stand-down memory

**The conflict.** This project's bots carry a memory that flags a rule after
2 consecutive losses and stands it down completely after 3 with the same
cause. That was ours, invented here.

**His rule is different in both halves, and better.**

*What counts as a streak:* two to three losing **WEEKS**, not days. In his
words, losing two trades is normal. Our 3-strikes trigger would have been
firing constantly on ordinary noise and switching off a working method.

*What you do about it:* **not trade less, and not size down — require MORE
evidence per trade.** Promote the entry bar from a bare sweep plus a break
of structure up to a sweep plus a break of structure PLUS an equilibrium
level or a fair value gap. He describes doing exactly this and what happened
the following week.

**Resolution: replace the stand-down memory with his escalation rule.** A
bad run tightens the filter rather than pulling the plug. This is a genuinely
better mechanism than the one we had, because switching a strategy off after
three losses guarantees you are switched off precisely when the losing run
ends.

## 9. His own evidence standard, which constrains how we test

Worth recording because it decides what we are allowed to show Wallace as
proof:

- **He rejects clicking through historical bars in a charting tool**, for a
  real reason: the higher timeframe candle is displayed already completed,
  so you are reading the future. That is a lookahead bug and he is right
  about it.
- **He counts only live and demo results as evidence.**
- What he calls backtesting is pattern drilling — roughly 10 examples per
  setup type, to train recognition, not to produce a number.

**What this means for us.** A properly coded walk-forward — where each bar
is decided using only bars that had closed at that moment, and every higher
timeframe is truncated to its last COMPLETED candle — does not have the flaw
he is objecting to. That is what our truncation tests exist to prove and
they must run on this build before any replay is shown as evidence.

So the week Wallace wants to see gets produced two ways, and both are
honest: a strictly causal replay of real recent data, and then the thing he
actually counts, a paper account trading it forward in real time.

## 10. Two different ways of deciding direction — use both, as a gate

**The conflict, and it is the sharpest one in the whole extraction.** He
TEACHES one method and PERFORMS a different one.

*Taught, in the current video:* read the previous session's shape — did
London consolidate, get pushed one way, or get pushed one way and reverse —
and take the direction from which of the three happened.

*Performed, on every live morning in the course:* walk the weekly chart down
through the daily, the 4-hour and the 1-hour, and **stand down when they
disagree**. His words: "at least I need the daily and the four hour to be in
confluence."

**Resolution: the current taught method sets the direction, and the
multi-timeframe agreement acts as a veto on top.** Direction comes from the
newer video, per the standing newer-wins rule. But the thing he does live is
a stand-down condition, it is cheap to compute, and it only ever REMOVES
trades. When the daily and the 4-hour disagree, there is no trade regardless
of what the session shape said.

Nothing is lost by requiring both. The failure mode of getting this wrong is
trading more, and trading more is exactly the failure this build must avoid.

## 11. THE NUMBER THAT TELLS US IF WE BUILT IT RIGHT

He states his own results: **a 64.29% win rate, average reward against risk
of 1 to 1.233, January to June 2026**, on roughly **7 to 15 trading days a
month.**

That last figure is the most useful number in all 310,000 words, and it is
the one to check the build against first.

There are about 21 trading days in a month. **He trades on a third to two
thirds of them and stands aside on the rest.** So:

> **If our build trades most days, we have dropped a stand-down condition.**

That is a concrete, falsifiable check on our implementation, and it is far
more sensitive than the win rate — a bug that drops one filter will show up
immediately as too many trades, long before it shows up in profit.

Same logic on the other numbers: a much higher win rate than 64% means we
have introduced lookahead somewhere. A much better reward-to-risk than
1:1.233 means our exit is seeing the future. **Beating his numbers is a bug
report, not a success.**
